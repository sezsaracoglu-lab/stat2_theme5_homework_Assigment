Course 2.1 · Theme 5 · Inference for regression

Every exam question is a patient walking in

You are not being asked to be clever. You are being asked to diagnose — to look at the words, name what kind of case this is, and then apply the one procedure that case always gets.

Sezen — this is the whole method, and it is only three steps

When you say you cannot tell what a question is asking, what is actually happening is that you are trying to do steps ① and ③ at the same time — reading the words while hunting for a formula. That never works, and it is not a failure of ability. It is a missing step in the middle.

A doctor never does that. Nobody looks at a patient and reaches straight for a prescription. They take the symptoms, they name the diagnosis, and only then does the treatment follow — because once you have the diagnosis, the treatment is not a decision any more. It is already written down.

Steps ① and ③ feel like the work. Step ② is the work — it is the only one that requires judgement, and it is the one the exam is really testing.

🩺 Why this beats hunting for a formula

What you have been doing

Read the question → feel lost → scan the formula sheet → find something with the right letters in it → hope.

This fails because several formulas contain the right letters. In Theme 5, “df” appears in four different places and means something different in each. The letters cannot tell you which one you need.

What to do instead

Read the question → name the case type before you look at anything → apply that type’s fixed procedure.

This works because there are only seven types, each has one procedure, and the words that identify a type are almost always in the last sentence of the stem.

★ The test of whether you have really diagnosed

You should be able to say the diagnosis out loud before you look at the answer options at all. If you cannot, do not look at them yet — reading the options first is how a good wrong answer catches you, because a well-written distractor is designed to look right to someone who has not diagnosed.

Case type 1 · appears in questions 1, 12, 16, 17, 18

🔢 Degrees of freedom

The single most common question type in Theme 5, and the easiest marks in the exam.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “The degrees of freedom for the t test are…” · “The degrees of freedom for the F test are…” · “how many degrees of freedom associated with error” · “degrees of freedom for regression”.
The −1 at the end is the intercept. You estimate p slopes plus one constant, so you spend p + 1 degrees of freedom out of n.
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Find n. From the story, or from Total df + 1.
  2. Find p — the number of predictors, not counting the constant.
  3. A t test on any coefficient → n − p − 1.
  4. An F test → quote p and n − p − 1, in that order.
  5. Asked for “df for regression”? That is just p.
🔁 Carries to: simple regression is the case p = 1, so n − p − 1 becomes n − 2. That is the only reason you ever saw n − 2 in Lecture 4 — it is not a separate rule.

⚠ The mis-diagnosis

With two predictors and 31 trees, df is 28, not 29. Writing n − 2 out of habit is the single most common error in this whole homework, and it costs you the t* lookup as well as the df mark.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 5 — the ANOVA table. Both involve the number df, and an ANOVA table prints df in it. The difference: type 1 asks you to produce a df from n and p; type 5 asks you to use a df that is already printed in order to get an MS. If the question says “the degrees of freedom … are”, it is type 1. If it says “the value of MS…”, the df is just an ingredient and you are on type 5.

Case type 2 · appears in questions 4, 8, 10, 19

📏 Critical values, and the confidence interval for a coefficient

Estimate, plus or minus a critical value times its own standard error — and, when the question asks for a cut-off instead, the critical value on its own.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “The 95% confidence interval for the slope is…” · “a 90% confidence interval for the coefficient of diameter” · or simply: every option contains a ±.
b ± t* · SE(b) t* comes from the t table with df = n − p − 1, at the confidence level asked for
The only thinking is which row and which confidence level. The rest is copying.
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Which row? The question names a variable — go to that row. “The slope” in a simple regression means the one predictor row, never Constant.
  2. Copy Coef → that is b.
  3. Copy SE Coef → that is SE(b).
  4. df = n − p − 1.
  5. Look up t* at the stated confidence level.
  6. Write b ± t*(SE). Stop — do not multiply unless the options are single numbers.
  7. If the question instead asks “reject H₀ if F > ?”, you want the F table at the stated α, entered with (p, n − p − 1) as in case type 1 — not the t table.
🔁 Carries to: the reverse question. If you are given an interval and asked about significance, check whether it contains zero. Contains zero → not significant at that level. Excludes zero → significant.

⚠ The mis-diagnosis

1.96 is never the answer in this course. It is the z value — the t* you would use with infinite df. With 26 or 28 degrees of freedom the correct multiplier is always a bit larger, because small samples deserve wider intervals. Any option containing 1.96 is bait.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 1 — degrees of freedom. Type 2 contains type 1: you cannot look up a critical value without the df. So when a question feels like type 2, do the type-1 step first and then carry on. The tell that you are on type 2 and not type 1 is that every option has a ± in it, or the stem says “reject H₀ if”.

Case type 3 · appears in question 3

🧱 Build the line from r, the means and the sums of squares

When they give you summary statistics instead of an equation.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “What is the equation of the least squares regression line?”, with r, x̄, ȳ, Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² and Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)² handed to you. Those five numbers are exactly and only what this case type needs.
b₁ = r · sy ⁄ sx = r · √( Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)² ⁄ Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² ) then b₀ = ȳ − b₁ x̄ the n − 1 in both standard deviations cancels, so you can go straight from the sums of squares
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Decide which variable is y. “the least squares fit of Volume on Height” means Volume is y. The thing named first, before “on”, is the response.
  2. Divide the two sums of squares — y's on top — and square-root it. That is sy/sx.
  3. Multiply by r → that is b₁.
  4. b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄. Walk back from the point of averages to x = 0.
  5. Check: put x̄ back in and you must land on ȳ.
🔁 Carries to: any question giving you r and two SDs instead of sums of squares — the formula is the same, you just skip the square root. And to the reverse: given b₁ and the two SDs, r = b₁ · sx/sy.

⚠ The mis-diagnosis

Two of the wrong options in Q3 are the x-on-y line, with the ratio upside down. Always ask: which variable am I predicting? Its spread goes on top.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 4 — predict, then residual. Both end with an equation. The difference is what you are handed. Type 3 hands you r, the means and the sums of squares and asks you to build the equation. Type 4 hands you the equation already built and asks you to use it. Look at what is given, not at what is asked.

Case type 4 · appears in questions 14, 15

🎯 Predict, then residual

Pure substitution — the only risk is arithmetic.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “What is the predicted…” · “what is the residual value for…”. If an equation and a set of values appear together, this is it.
ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂ + …  ·  residual = observed y − predicted ŷ observed first. Always.
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Match each value to its x. Write the pairs out in a column before multiplying — this is where marks are lost, not in the arithmetic.
  2. Multiply each coefficient by its value. Watch the units: if x₃ is “faculty compensation” and the study measured it in dollars, plug in 46000, not 46.
  3. Add everything, including the intercept, respecting the signs.
  4. For a residual: observed − predicted. A negative answer means the case sat below the model's prediction.
🔁 Carries to: every prediction question in the course, simple or multiple. And note the sign: a residual of −2448.73 says this university charges $2,448.73 less than the model expected. That sentence is often the mark.

⚠ The mis-diagnosis

The distractor +2448.73 is the subtraction the wrong way round — and it reverses the meaning, saying the university is more expensive than expected. The ±3447.92 pair have no route from these numbers at all. If you can compute a residual and say which side of the line the point falls on, neither can catch you.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 3 — build the line. Same pair as above, the other way round. If the equation is printed in the stem, you are on type 4 and no SDs are needed. And within type 4 itself, keep the two halves apart: predicted is the substitution, residual is the subtraction that follows it.

Case type 5 · appears in questions 9, 11, 20, 23

🥧 Fill in the ANOVA table

Four numbers and three relationships. Give me any table with gaps and I can fill it.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “The value of the MSregression is…” · “the value of the F statistic is…” · “the value of the multiple R² is…” · any table printed with a blank column.
The tree data, filled in. Green = divide across, gold = divide down, blue = the R² shortcut.
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Asked for an MS? Divide that row's SS by that row's df. MSM = 7684.2 ÷ 2 = 3842.1
  2. Asked for F? Divide MSM by MSE — you may have to compute both first. F = 3842.1 ÷ 15.07 = 254.95
  3. Asked for R²? SSM ÷ SST. R² = 7684.2 ÷ 8106.1 = 0.948
  4. Missing a SS? The column adds: SST = SSM + SSE.
🔁 Carries to: the standard error of the estimate, which is just √MSE — here √15.07 = 3.88. And to recovering n: Total df + 1.

⚠ The mis-diagnosis

Quoting a sum of squares where a mean square was asked for. In Q9 the option “7684.2” is right there, and it is the SS, not the MS. The word is always Mean Square — an average, so something got divided.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 1 — degrees of freedom. An ANOVA table is mostly df, so type-1 instincts fire here. But type 5 is bookkeeping, not derivation: the numbers you need are almost all already on the page, and the work is knowing that SS and df add downward while MS and F are divisions across.

Case type 6 · appears in questions 6, 21, 24

⚖️ Is this predictor pulling its weight?

Two different tests, and the exam loves the gap between them.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “which variables do not significantly contribute…” · “the P-value for the F test was p = 0.001. This means that…” · “we may conclude that…”.
Once df is above about 20, the 5% critical t sits between 1.96 and 2.09 — 2.086 at df = 20, falling to 1.977 at df = 137 — so “is |t| past 2?” is a reliable exam shortcut.
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Whole model or one predictor? The word “F” means whole model; a named variable means that variable's t.
  2. For one predictor: read its t, compare |t| with about 2 (or its p with α).
  3. List the ones that fail — the question usually asks which do not contribute.
  4. Before answering a “we may conclude” option, ask whether it smuggles in a claim about a different model or about causation.
🔁 Carries to: the bear data in Q24 — five t values, and you simply sort them either side of 2. Head length (−1.51), head width (−0.18) and body length (1.08) fail; neck girth (4.83) and chest girth (10.37) pass.

⚠ The mis-diagnosis

A significant F does not mean all the slopes are non-zero — only that at least one is. And a predictor being significant here does not mean it would be significant on its own, nor that the others can be dropped. Any option that slides between models is wrong.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 7 — read the output. Both are judgement questions about a printout, and both use the phrase “we may conclude that”. The difference: type 6 is about one predictor’s own row — its t, its p, is it earning its place. Type 7 is about the model as a whole — is a line the right shape, does this show a cause, what does R² mean. If the question names a specific variable, it is type 6.

Case type 7 · appears in questions 2, 5, 7, 13, 22, 25

👁️ Read the picture, or spot the false claim

No arithmetic. These are the “do you actually understand it” questions.

🩺 ① Symptoms — the words that give it away. “Which of the following statements is FALSE?” · “Does it indicate any problems?” · “We may conclude that…” · “How many parameters…” · “The explanatory variable(s) is/are…”.
Q25’s plot is the middle one turned upside down — a U rather than an arch. Either way the centre of the cloud is moving, and that is what curvature means.
③ The treatment — and it never varies
  1. Residual plot? Look at the centre line first. Drifting → curvature. Flat but widening → nonconstant variance.
  2. “Which is FALSE?” Check the formulas character by character. The usual plant is n − 1 where it should be n − 2.
  3. “We may conclude?” Reject anything claiming a good fit proves validity, or that R² is an accuracy percentage, or that a result carries over to a different model.
  4. Counting parameters: p slopes + 1 intercept = p + 1.
  5. Explanatory variables: everything you measured to predict with — never the response.
🔁 Carries to: the intervals question. A confidence interval for a mean response is narrowest at the middle of the data and fans out at both ends, because the line pivots on the point of averages.

🔀 Differential — the type this gets confused with

Type 6 — is this predictor pulling its weight?. As above. Type 7 is also the default when nothing else fits: if the question has no numbers to compute and asks what you may conclude, it is almost always type 7, and the answer is almost always the humblest option on the list.

🎴 The cheat card

If you memorise nothing else from Theme 5, memorise this page.

① The opening move, every single time

Read the last sentence of the stem and find the noun it asks for: degrees of freedom · confidence interval · the equation · the predicted value · the residual · MS · F · we may conclude. That noun is the diagnosis. Say it before you look at the options.

② Degrees of freedom — the whole of case type 1

n = how many cases · p = how many predictors (not counting the intercept)
df for error / for a t test on any coefficient = n − p − 1
df for regression = p
df total = n − 1  ·  and it checks: p + (n − p − 1) = n − 1
Simple regression is just p = 1, so n − p − 1 becomes n − 2. That is not a separate rule.

③ Confidence intervals — case type 2

b ± t* × SE(b), where t* comes from the t table with n − p − 1 df.
95% two-sided → the 0.025 column. 90% → the 0.05 column. 99% → the 0.005 column.
Contains zero → not significant at that level. Excludes zero → significant. That equivalence is worth a mark on its own.
Asked for a cut-off instead (“reject H₀ if F > …”)? Then it is the F table at that α, entered with (p, n − p − 1).

④ The ANOVA table — case type 5, and it is pure bookkeeping

Down the columns: SSM + SSE = SST, and dfM + dfE = dfT.
Across the rows: MS = SS ÷ df.
F = MSM ÷ MSE.
R² = SSM ÷ SST — and SST is SSM + SSE, never SSE on its own.
s = √MSE, the standard error of the estimate.
Give me any ANOVA table with gaps and these five lines fill it.

⑤ Two different tests, and the exam lives in the gap

The F test asks one question about the whole model: is at least one slope non-zero? A small p says “something in here matters” — never “everything in here matters”.
Each t test asks about one predictor: does it earn its place given that every other predictor is already in the model? That last clause is the entire difficulty of multiple regression.
So a predictor can be significant on its own and useless in the model, and the reverse. Never read one test as if it were the other.

⑥ What a printout can never tell you — case type 7

A big R² and a tiny p say the model fits. They cannot say:
that a line was the right shape — only the residual plot can;
that x causes y — only a designed experiment can;
that individual predictions will be accurate — R² is about variation explained, not about any one case;
that the model is “validated” — that word is a trap wherever it appears.
When an option sounds confident and an option sounds cautious, the cautious one is usually right.

★ The one sentence that unlocks Theme 5

Every number on a regression printout is either something you can derive from two other numbers, or a judgement the printout cannot make for you. Case types 1–5 are the first kind: pure arithmetic once diagnosed. Case types 6 and 7 are the second: no arithmetic will save you, and the humble answer is usually the right one.

🧪 Exam B

The same seven case types, pushed harder: the symptoms are less obvious, several questions make you do two case types in sequence, and the judgement questions offer you a statement that is perfectly true but does not answer what was asked. Sit this one after you have cleared Exam A.

What makes this one harder. In Exam A the symptom usually points straight at one case type. Here you will meet stems that look like one type and are another — a question that mentions “degrees of freedom” but is really asking for a critical value, a printout question that is really about causation. Diagnose before you compute, every time.
